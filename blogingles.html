Natural phenomena

What they are, how to protect yourself, and how to prevent them.

Climate Changes

It refers to long-term shifts in temperature patterns and meteorological phenomena on the planet. Although Earth has gone through several natural climate cycles throughout its history, caused by factors such as volcanic activity or variations in solar orbit, the current pace of warming has accelerated drastically over the last 150 years.


Since the Industrial Revolution, the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and gas has released large volumes of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide and methane. These gases form a layer that traps the sun's heat, and while this greenhouse effect is a natural and essential process to keep Earth warm, its intensification by human activities and deforestation prevents excess heat from escaping into space.


As a consequence of this imbalance, global warming causes more frequent extreme weather events, sea level rise, glacier melting, and severe threats to biodiversity and food security. UN scientific reports indicate that limiting the global temperature increase to a maximum of 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels is crucial to avoid the worst impacts and ensure a habitable climate, but current projections warn that the planet could reach about 3.2°C of warming by the end of the century if more drastic measures are not taken.

Rain

A fundamental meteorological phenomenon that is part of Earth's water cycle, consisting of the precipitation of liquid water droplets falling from clouds to the Earth's surface. This process begins when water from oceans, rivers, lakes, and soil evaporates due to heating caused by the sun, turning into water vapor that rises into the atmosphere.


As this warm air vapor rises, it cools and condenses upon contact with colder layers, gathering around small particles suspended in the air to form clouds. When these water droplets increase in size and become too heavy to be supported by upward air currents, they fall in the form of rain, playing an essential role in replenishing water resources and maintaining life and ecosystems on the planet.

Earthquakes

Also known as seismic tremors, are sudden shakes or vibrations that occur on the Earth's surface due to the sudden release of energy accumulated in the lithosphere, the planet's outer rocky layer. This energy propagates in all directions in the form of seismic waves, which make the ground vibrate and can cause anything from small, imperceptible tremors to major destruction depending on their intensity.


The main engine behind this phenomenon is the dynamics of tectonic plates, which are huge rocky blocks that make up the Earth's crust and float on the semi-molten mantle. Since these plates are in constant slow and continuous motion, they frequently collide, slide laterally, or pull apart from one another, generating enormous friction and pressure along geological faults. When this accumulated tension exceeds the strength of the rocks, they suddenly break or shift, releasing the accumulated energy and triggering the earthquake.

El Niño

Is a natural climatic phenomenon characterized by the abnormal warming of surface waters in the central and eastern regions of the equatorial Pacific Ocean. It is part of a global cycle that alters atmospheric circulation when trade winds weaken, allowing warm water masses to accumulate near the coast of South America instead of moving toward Oceania and Asia.


This oceanic shift causes drastic impacts on the climate of various regions around the planet, triggering severe droughts and heatwaves in areas such as northern Brazil, Australia, and southern Africa. In contrast, the phenomenon tends to intensify rainfall and increase the risk of flooding in South American countries like Uruguay and the southern region of Brazil, profoundly altering agricultural regimes and global ecosystems.

Tsunamis

Is a series of gigantic and extremely destructive waves generated by the sudden displacement of a large volume of water in the ocean. The most common causes for this phenomenon include violent submarine earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or large underwater landslides, which abruptly push the water mass upward.


In the open sea, these waves can go almost unnoticed due to their low height, but they travel at impressive speeds that can exceed seven hundred kilometers per hour. However, as they approach the coast and encounter shallow waters, their speed drops drastically, causing the accumulated energy to transform into a colossal wall of water capable of flooding vast land areas.


It is worth noting that a tsunami is rarely limited to a single wave, generally consisting of a sequence of violent peaks separated by minutes or even hours, with the first wave not always being the most dangerous. Natural signs such as a strong coastal earthquake or a sudden and drastic retreat of the tide serve as vital warnings for immediate evacuation toward higher ground.
